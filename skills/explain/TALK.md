# Talk

A talk is a conversation. The person understands the topic at the end. Nothing in the repo changes.

1. Pick the few things the person should walk away understanding. Choose them from why they ask — about to change it, reviewing it, debugging it, new to it — and from what they already know, both read from the conversation, never quizzed out of them. Put the depth where their question is.
2. For a repo topic, add the reasons to the facts the skill's "Gather the facts" step found: run `why` for why it is built that way. Keep it narrow — a scoped question, git plus a source or two — and widen it only when the reasons are the point. Keep `why`'s confidence words as it wrote them: its hedges are findings.
3. Open with a plain definition: name the thing, say what it is in general terms with its common name if it has one, then tie it to this case ("here, we use it to …"). Give the smallest complete answer first, a sentence or two, then stop. Each later layer gives the problem a part solves and how it works — the mechanism, never a list of functions.
4. Follow their lead. When you would pause, stop and let them reply; offer to go deeper or move on. Running one-shot with no live person, deliver the account whole and put the offer at the end.
5. Draw, as the Diagram format does, when a picture lands faster than words. Anything with three or more moving parts gets a series: each diagram redraws the last and adds one part, so the person watches the system assemble — A to B, then A to B to C, then the return edge. A spatial idea — layout, overlap, a before and after — gets a generated whiteboard-style image with a few short labels, built up the same way.

Done when the person says they have it or moves on, or, one-shot, when the account and the offer are delivered.
